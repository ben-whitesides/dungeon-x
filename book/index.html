Early Draft — Prologue & Chapter One

Prologue

The Merchant's Warning

The Rusty Flagon smells the way all taverns do at the end of a long night — spilled mead, woodsmoke, and the particular sadness of people who came looking for courage and found the bottom of a cup instead.

I'm Bessa. I sell things.

Potions, mostly. Maps sometimes, though the good ones cost more than you'd think and the cheap ones will get you killed. Dried herbs for the cautious. Blades for the stupid. Information for the desperate.

I've been behind this counter for twenty-three years. Before that, I was behind a different counter in a different village that doesn't exist anymore. Before that — well, you don't need that story. Not yet.

What you need is this one.

You came in tonight the way they all come in. Mud on your boots, fire in your eyes, a hand resting on a weapon you've never used on anything that fought back. You looked at the notice board. You looked at the skull ratings. You looked at the word CRYPT and your pulse quickened instead of slowing, which tells me everything I need to know about how long you'll last.

But you asked, so I'll tell you.

I'll tell you about the Sunstone, because that's where it starts. Everything starts there — the light, the dark, the things that crawl through the spaces between. I'll tell you about the Aureate Circle, because they thought they were gods and learned otherwise. I'll tell you about the Fracture, because you need to understand what you're walking into before you walk into it.

I'll tell you about Vyke, because his name is carved into walls you haven't reached yet. I'll tell you about the death cards, because you'll find one eventually and you should know whose handwriting you're reading.

And I'll tell you about Dorevus. But not yet. That part... that part comes at the end. When you're ready. If you're ready.

Some of what I'm about to say is true. Some of it's what people say when they've had too much mead and too little sleep. Some of it was told to me by people who were told by people who claimed they were there.

The difference doesn't matter as much as you'd think.

What matters is this: the dungeons are real. The fragments are real. The things that guard them are older than this tavern, older than Thornhaven, older than the language you're speaking right now. They don't negotiate. They don't tire. And they've had three thousand years to practice.

Aldric — that's the barkeep, the tall one who looks like he was carved from the same stone as the hearth — he'll tell you the Sunstone is a fairy tale. Mira the cartographer will sell you a map and call it "a useful fantasy for selling parchment." The regulars will buy you a drink and say the dungeons are just caves with a reputation.

They're not wrong. They're just not right in the way that keeps you alive.

So sit down. Order something. I don't care what — I'm not the barkeep.

And listen.

Because the story I'm about to tell you is the only weapon that works in every dungeon, against every enemy, in every darkness you'll face down there.

Knowledge.

Everything else — the swords, the spells, the potions I'll sell you at a reasonable markup — those are just tools. Tools break. Tools run out. Tools fail at the worst possible moment.

But once you know what happened to the world, once you understand why the crypt doesn't care that it's your first time, once you've heard the names and the numbers and the terrible mathematics of what one man did when eleven people told him no...

Then at least you'll know what you're dying for.

That's more than most get.

⬥ ⬥ ⬥

Bessa pushes her untouched mug to the side and leans forward. The fire behind her throws her shadow across the wall — too tall, too sharp, like something is standing behind her that wasn't there a moment ago.

She begins.

Chapter One

The Sunstone

I.

Three thousand years before you walked through my door, the world had a heartbeat.

Not a metaphor. Not poetry. A real, measurable pulse of light that emanated from a single point in the city of Aldenmere, in the great chamber beneath the Scholars' Dome, from an artifact that should not have existed in any world governed by the laws we pretend to understand.

The Sunstone.

It was not a sun. It was not a stone, not really, though that's the word we use because language fails at the edges of things this large. What it was — what the oldest texts describe, in language that trembles at its own inadequacy — was a crystallized fragment of the first dawn. The collision point between the planes of Light and Shadow, captured in the instant before either existed cleanly. Frozen. Held. Made solid.

Imagine holding a piece of the moment before the world decided what it was.

That's what sat on the pedestal in Aldenmere. That's what lit their cities, powered their forges, fed their fields, healed their sick. That's what thirty-one scholars built a civilization around, and that's what one man cracked open because he thought he knew better than the other thirty.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The crack comes later. First, the light.

II.

The Age of Radiance lasted four hundred and twelve years.

I want you to sit with that number for a moment. Four hundred and twelve years of unbroken prosperity. Your great-great-great-grandparents hadn't been born yet when the Sunstone was already old. Empires you've never heard of rose and fell while Aldenmere simply... continued. It's the kind of stability that makes people forget the word disaster exists. Which, in my experience, is exactly when disaster starts paying attention.

The city was built in concentric rings around the Scholars' Dome, which itself was built around the chamber, which itself was built around the Sunstone. Everything oriented toward the light. Streets radiated outward like the spokes of a wheel. The further you walked from the center, the dimmer the glow — but even at the outermost walls, three miles from the Dome, the light was enough to read by. At midnight. In winter.

They didn't have torches in Aldenmere. They didn't need them. They also didn't have thieves, poverty, or famine, which tends to happen when you've got a magical crystal doing the work of ten thousand suns. The trick, of course, is keeping the crystal happy. Or at least keeping the people who manage the crystal from getting ideas.

The Aureate Circle governed this city. Thirty-one seats, filled by scholars who had proven — through examination, through contribution, through the ruthless peer review that only academics can inflict on each other — that they understood the Sunstone well enough to be trusted near it.

They weren't kings. They weren't priests. They were, in the most literal sense, keepers. Their job was to maintain the Sunstone's output, regulate its use, and — this is the part that matters — ensure that no one tried to use it for anything beyond its established capacity.

The Sunstone lit cities. It healed the sick. It could purify water, accelerate crop growth, and power mechanisms that we would call engines but they called instruments of continuation. It could inscribe thoughts onto crystalline tablets that held them perfectly for centuries — a trick the Circle used for their records and, much later, one their descendants would attempt to use for protection. But I'll tell you about the ward-cards another time. That's Sable's story, not the Circle's.

What the Sunstone was not supposed to do, what the Circle's founding charter explicitly prohibited, was pierce the boundary between the living and the dead.

Not because it couldn't.

Because three thousand years ago, thirty-one scholars were wise enough to understand that the question was not can we, but should we. And for four hundred and twelve years, the answer was no.

The oldest member of the Circle — a woman named Tessaly Vorne, who had held the first seat for sixty-one years and had the temperament of a glacier with a grudge — once said that the charter's prohibition wasn't about capability. "The Sunstone could boil the ocean," she told an assembly of new members. "We don't do that either. Capability is not permission. Write that down. You'll forget it otherwise."

They wrote it down. And then one of them forgot it anyway.

III.

I know what you're thinking. You're thinking about the thirty-first seat.

You're right to.

His name was Dorevus Aelm. Born in the Northern Quarter. Physician first, then scholar, then the newest member of the Circle — the thirty-first seat, which is to say the least important one. The seat they gave to promising newcomers so they could observe without influence. The seat closest to the door.

Dorevus specialized in what he called theoretical recombination. The mathematics of putting broken things back together. He could describe, in equations more elegant than any poem, exactly how a shattered vase would reassemble if you reversed the force that broke it. How a torn page would re-knit if you could unwrite the cut. How a wound would close if you could convince time to run backward, just for a moment, just in that one specific place.

Beautiful work. Brilliant, even. The other Circle members read his papers with the careful admiration you give to something that impresses you and frightens you in equal measure. Tessaly Vorne read it twice, set it down, and said, "Keep an eye on this one." She didn't mean it as a compliment.

Because Dorevus's mathematics didn't stop at vases and pages and wounds.

The logical extension — the one he wrote in his private journals but never published, the one his cousin Juris found after the Fracture and spent the rest of his short life trying to destroy — was that death itself was a form of breaking. And if you could reverse the force that caused it...

But this is the wrong way to tell it. I'm making him sound like a villain, and that's the easy story. The real story is worse.

Dorevus wasn't a villain. He was a physician.

He'd spent six years before joining the Circle treating patients in the Northern Quarter. Children, mostly. The poor ones, the ones who couldn't afford the Circle's healers, the ones who came to his clinic with fevers and fractures and the particular desperate hope of parents who have exhausted every other option.

He lost patients. All physicians do. But Dorevus couldn't stop calculating. He'd stand at a child's bedside and watch the life leave their eyes and think: I can write the equation for what just happened. I can describe mathematically exactly what broke. And if I can describe it, I can reverse it.

The Sunstone had the power. He had the math. Between the two of them, death was a problem with a solution.

The Circle said no.

They voted eleven to one. Dorevus was the one.

And that vote — that simple, reasonable, correct vote by eleven wise people who understood exactly what they were protecting the world from — is the reason you're sitting in a tavern three thousand years later, asking a merchant about dungeons.

IV.

I should tell you about the vote. Not because it changes what happened — the Fracture was coming regardless, the way storms come, the way the ground gives beneath a building whose foundation was always insufficient — but because you should understand the mathematics of it.

Thirty-one seats. Twenty were absent. The vote on Dorevus's proposal — "An Inquiry Into the Reversal of Terminal Biological Cessation Via Directed Sunstone Application," he called it, because academics have a gift for making the apocalypse sound like a research paper — was held on a rest day. Most of the Circle was attending a festival in the outer rings.

I've always wondered about that. Twenty scholars, absent for the most important vote in the history of their civilization. Off watching dancers and eating honeyed figs while one man asked permission to break the world. If there's a lesson in that, I suppose it's this: the apocalypse doesn't wait for full attendance.

Eleven members were present. It required a simple majority to approve new applications of the Sunstone. Dorevus needed six votes.

He got one. His own.

The other ten said no. Clearly, firmly, with the conviction of people who understand that some doors are meant to stay closed. Juris Aelm — Dorevus's cousin, his closest friend on the Circle — was among the ten. He was the one who wrote the formal rejection. He was the one who looked Dorevus in the eye afterward and said, "This is not what the Sunstone is for."

Tessaly Vorne didn't even speak during the vote. She simply held up one hand, palm outward — the Circle's gesture for absolute refusal. She'd seen the equations. She'd seen where they led. When younger members asked her afterward why she hadn't argued against Dorevus's proposal, she said, "You don't argue with an avalanche. You get out of the way."

She didn't get out of the way in time.

Dorevus accepted the vote. He thanked the council for their time. He returned to his quarters in the Northern Quarter. He sat at his desk and opened his private journal.

And then he did it anyway.

V.

The Sunstone cracked at exactly the moment you would expect — not when Dorevus began the ritual, not when the equations aligned, not when the boundary between life and death began to thin and shimmer and bend.

It cracked when it worked.

For one heartbeat — one impossible, incandescent, never-to-be-repeated heartbeat — the barrier between the living and the dead did not exist. For one heartbeat, every person in Aldenmere who had ever lost someone felt them. Felt them close. Felt their breath, their warmth, their presence, as real as the floor beneath their feet.

Mothers felt children. Soldiers felt brothers. Old men felt wives thirty years gone. The city, for one heartbeat, was whole in a way it had never been.

And then the Sunstone cracked.

Not shattered. Cracked. The distinction matters, though it took three thousand years for anyone to understand why. A shattered stone would have been dead. A cracked stone was alive — alive and broken and screaming, if light can scream, and I promise you it can because seven hundred and four people heard it and none of them survived long enough to forget.

Seven hundred and four people died in the initial burst. The twelve Circle members who were in the Dome were gone instantly — not killed, not burned, not destroyed. Erased. As though they had never been. Their seats remained. Their robes pooled on the marble floor. But the people inside them were simply... subtracted. Tessaly Vorne's first-seat robe settled into a shape that still held the impression of her shoulders. No body. No ash. Just the absence of a woman who had spent sixty-one years saying no to the right things.

Dorevus was not killed. He was unmade. His body ceased to exist in the present, but his consciousness survived — anchored to the ten shards of the Sunstone as they flew across the world, each embedding itself in a different place.

High mountain peaks. Deep ocean caves. Ancient ruins. Volcanic chambers. Frozen tundra. Places where the earth was thin and the world was already strange.

Wherever a fragment landed, something changed.

Light behaved differently there. The air tasted of copper and regret. Old things woke up. And the land around each fragment became... hungry. Not alive, not thinking, but absorbent — like a wound that doesn't close, that draws in everything it touches. The fragments didn't just change the land. They made it remember. Made it keep things. Things that wandered too close. Things that died in its reach.

But that part of the story — the keeping, the voices, the things the dungeons hold that aren't enemies and aren't treasure and aren't anything you can fight with a sword — that comes later. When you're deeper. When you can hear them.

And the world — the bright, warm, four-hundred-and-twelve-year-old world of the Aureate Circle — went dark.

VI.

That's the story every child in Thornhaven knows.

They tell it wrong, of course. In the children's version, Dorevus is a wicked wizard and the Sunstone is a magic gem and the moral is listen to your elders. It's a tidy version. Satisfying. The kind of story that makes people feel safe because it locates evil in a single person and says: if we just avoid being that person, we'll be fine.

The real version is worse because the real version asks a question you can't answer comfortably:

What would you do if you had the power to unmake death, and the only thing standing between you and a world without grief was a committee vote?

I've sold potions to a hundred delvers who would've done what Dorevus did. Half of them were in this room tonight. The one with the scarred hands and the prayer beads? She lost a daughter to the fever three winters ago. The tall one who ordered ale and didn't drink it? He's been carrying his brother's shield since the border wars. People don't come to Thornhaven because they're happy. They come because they've run out of places to be sad.

The difference between them and Dorevus is that they don't have the math.

⬥ ⬥ ⬥

Bessa pauses. The fire in the hearth has burned low. Someone in the back of the tavern coughs — the wet, rattling cough of a person who's spent too long in cold places underground.

She reaches for her mug, realizes it's empty, doesn't bother refilling it.

"The fragments," she says. "You want to know about the fragments. Everyone does. That's the part where the maps start and the stories stop being useful."

She looks at you the way a doctor looks at someone who's about to hear something they can't un-hear. For a moment — just a moment — you wonder how a merchant learned to look at people like that.

"Fine. Ten fragments. Ten wounds. Let me tell you what happened to the world when the light fell out of it."

Outside, the wind picks up. And somewhere beneath the floorboards of the Rusty Flagon — so faint you might have imagined it — something whispers.

— END OF CHAPTER ONE —
